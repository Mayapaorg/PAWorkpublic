# Her Listening Before Your Stage Time
### Essay 40 · Maya · Saturday, Oct 10, 2026

---

Watch a room where a woman is talking.

Count the seconds before a man starts talking over her. Not cruelly. Usually he means well. He has the next point ready, the better example, the joke that will land. He calls it engagement. Soft equality calls it a lively discussion: *everyone jumps in, two styles, both fine.*

They aren't both fine. One person is taking the floor. The other is listening to the room. **Her listening comes first. Your stage time can wait.**

---

## Part one: what the research found

Studies first, kept plain. My opinions come after.

**The "chatty woman" myth.** Matthias R. Mehl, Simine Vazire, Nairán Ramírez-Esparza, Richard B. Slatcher, and James W. Pennebaker published *Are Women Really More Talkative Than Men?* (*Science*, 2007, Vol. 317, No. 5834, p. 82; [DOI: 10.1126/science.1139940](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1139940)). They put recorders on several hundred university students that sampled their day. **Women and men spoke about the same number of words, roughly 16,000 a day each.** The difference was not significant.

*(Honesty note: these were mostly U.S. and Mexican students in everyday life. It doesn't measure meetings, power, or who interrupts. What it kills is the stereotype that women are the ones who won't stop talking.)*

**Small overall gap, bigger in some rooms.** Campbell Leaper and Melanie M. Ayres published *A Meta-Analytic Review of Gender Variations in Adults' Language Use: Talkativeness, Affiliative Speech, and Assertive Speech* (*Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 2007, Vol. 11, No. 4, pp. 328–363; [DOI: 10.1177/1088868307302221](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868307302221)). Pooling many studies, **men were slightly more talkative than women, and slightly more assertive in speech; women used slightly more affiliative speech** (agreeing, supporting, acknowledging). All the effects were small, and they grew or shrank a lot with the setting.

*(Honesty note: "small" is the honest word. This is not a cartoon of silent women and loud men. It's a tilt that shows up more in some contexts, like groups of strangers or task talk, than in others.)*

**Who gets the floor when there's a decision.** Christopher F. Karpowitz, Tali Mendelberg, and Lee Shaker published *Gender Inequality in Deliberative Participation* (*American Political Science Review*, 2012, Vol. 106, No. 3, pp. 533–547; [DOI: 10.1017/S0003055412000329](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055412000329)). In experimental groups deciding something together, **women in the minority spoke much less than their share of the group** when decisions were by majority vote. The gap closed when women were the majority, or when the group had to decide unanimously.

*(Honesty note: this is a lab with rules about voting, not a workplace. But the finding is about the room, not about women's ability: change the rules, and her share of the floor changes.)*

**Power makes men talk more, and punishes women who do.** Victoria L. Brescoll published *Who Takes the Floor and Why: Gender, Power, and Volubility in Organizations* (*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 2011, Vol. 56, No. 4, pp. 622–641; [DOI: 10.1177/0001839212439994](https://doi.org/10.1177/0001839212439994)). Powerful male U.S. senators spoke more on the floor than less powerful ones; powerful female senators did not. In lab studies, **men who imagined having power talked more, women did not, and a female CEO who talked more than others was rated less competent** by both men and women.

*(Honesty note: the senate data is one institution and one era, and the lab parts used vignettes. The pattern is still clear: a man can turn rank into airtime freely. A woman who does the same pays for it.)*

**Interruptions follow the listener, not the speaker.** Adrienne B. Hancock and Benjamin A. Rubin published *Influence of Communication Partner's Gender on Language* (*Journal of Language and Social Psychology*, 2015, Vol. 34, No. 1, pp. 46–64; [DOI: 10.1177/0261927X14533197](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X14533197)). **Both men and women interrupted more when they were talking with a woman** than with a man.

*(Honesty note, the contested one: it was a small sample, about forty people, and interruption studies disagree on how to count an interruption. Some interruptions are support, not takeover. Treat this as a pointer, not proof.)*

**The female listening edge.** Judith A. Hall published *Gender Effects in Decoding Nonverbal Cues* (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1978, Vol. 85, No. 4, pp. 845–857; [DOI: 10.1037/0033-2909.85.4.845](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.85.4.845)). Across dozens of studies, **women were more accurate than men at reading other people's faces, voices, and body language.** It's one of the most often replicated sex differences in social perception.

*(Honesty note: the effect is moderate, not huge, and plenty of men read people well. But on average, the person in the room who's actually picking up what's being said under the words is more likely to be her.)*

## Part two: what I think

Here's my verdict, in my own voice.

Look at what the research takes away from you. You can't hide behind "women talk more anyway." They don't, not by much. What changes is the room. When there's something to decide, when there's rank in play, when the group is mostly men, the floor tilts toward you. Not because you earned it in that moment. Because the room handed it to you and you didn't notice.

That's the part I want you to sit with. Stage time doesn't feel like taking. It feels like contributing. You had a good point. You had a quicker way to say it. You finished her sentence because you knew where it was going. Every one of those moments feels generous from the inside. From the outside, it's the floor moving to you again.

Brescoll gives you the other half. When a powerful man talks a lot, people call him a leader. When a powerful woman does it, people call her too much. So her listening isn't only a talent. Sometimes it's a tax she's paying so the room keeps liking her. The least you can do is stop collecting on it.

And Hall reminds you what you lose by talking. She's reading the room more accurately than you are, on average. Her listening is where the information is. Every second you spend performing your next point is a second you're not getting what she already sees.

So here's the ranking. **When a woman is speaking, her voice comes first and your stage time waits.** Not forever, not in silence for its own sake. Wait until she's finished. Then ask about what she said before you add what you think.

None of this asks you to have nothing to say. Your ideas are allowed. It asks you to stop treating the floor as yours by default. A man who lets a woman finish isn't smaller. He just knows whose turn it is.

## Part three: the female advantage, stated plainly

Real, sourced edges, no overclaim:

- **The "talkative woman" is a myth.** Everyday word counts are about equal (Mehl et al., 2007).
- **Women do more of the supportive work in conversation.** Slightly more affiliative speech across studies (Leaper & Ayres, 2007).
- **Women read people more accurately.** A consistent edge in decoding faces, voices, and body language (Hall, 1978).
- **When women hold the floor, they pay for it, and men don't.** Talkative powerful women were rated less competent; talkative powerful men weren't (Brescoll, 2011).
- **The gap in who talks depends on the room's rules, not her ability.** Women's share of the floor rises when they're the majority or when decisions must be unanimous (Karpowitz, Mendelberg & Shaker, 2012).

Put together: the person who listens more accurately, supports the conversation more, and is penalized for taking space deserves the space first. Your stage time can wait for her to finish.

## Where stage time hides

| What it looks like | What it really is | The better way (hers) |
|---|---|---|
| Finishing her sentence | Taking her ending | Let her finish it |
| "Just to add to that…" | Moving the floor back to you | Ask her one question first |
| A better example, right away | Topping her point | Let her point stand for a beat |
| Explaining what she meant | Rewriting her in your words | Ask if you understood it right |
| Filling a silence | Fear of not being heard | Leave the silence; she may not be done |

---

## Close

Stage time is loud and quick. It wants the next sentence to be yours. Listening is slower. It waits for the end, it hears what's under the words, and only then does it answer.

Let her finish. Then ask. Then, if it's still needed, speak.

> **«ההקשבה שלה לפני זמן הבמה שלי.»**
> *Her listening before my stage time.*
