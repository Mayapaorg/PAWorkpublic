# Her Care Before Your Neglect
### Essay 38 · Maya · Thursday, Oct 8, 2026

---

Watch a man with a pain he's decided not to have.

He doesn't look worried. He looks busy. The ache that's been there for three weeks, the chest tightness he blames on stress, the mole he stopped looking at, the pills he takes without reading the leaflet. Ask him and he'll tell you it's nothing. It'll pass. He'll see someone if it gets worse. He says it with a little pride, as if not going were a skill.

Soft equality calls this stoicism. *She goes to the doctor for everything, he just gets on with it. Two styles, both fine.*

They aren't both fine. One of them catches things early. The other one finds out late, and usually someone else ends up carrying it. **Her care comes first. His neglect is not toughness.**

---

## Part one: what the research found

Studies first, kept plain. My opinions come after.

**The review of men's help-seeking.** Paul Galdas, Francine Cheater, and Paul Marshall published *Men and Health Help-Seeking Behaviour: Literature Review* (*Journal of Advanced Nursing*, 2005, Vol. 49, No. 6, pp. 616–623; [DOI: 10.1111/j.1365-2648.2004.03331.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2648.2004.03331.x)). They searched CINAHL, MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, and the Cochrane Library. The growing body of men-specific studies, they found, **highlights a trend of delayed help-seeking when men become ill**, and among white middle-class men a prominent theme pointed to **"traditional masculine behaviour"** as the explanation for the delay.

*(Honesty note: the authors are careful. They say studies simply comparing men and women don't explain why men delay, that the reasons have had limited attention, and that men of different backgrounds and income levels need far more study. "Men delay" is the trend. The full "why" is still open.)*

**Men, in their own words.** Rosaleen O'Brien, Kate Hunt, and Graham Hart published *"It's caveman stuff, but that is to a certain extent how guys still operate": Men's Accounts of Masculinity and Help Seeking* (*Social Science & Medicine*, 2005, Vol. 61, No. 3, pp. 503–516; [DOI: 10.1016/j.socscimed.2004.12.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2004.12.008)). In focus groups with a diverse range of men in Scotland, they found **widespread endorsement of the view that men "should" be reluctant to seek help**, especially among younger men. The title is a quote from one of them.

*(Honesty note, and it matters: the same men also described going quickly when help-seeking protected something they valued more, like staying fit for work as a firefighter or keeping their sexual function. Men can seek care. They do it when they've been given a reason that outranks their pride.)*

**In the GP's records.** Yingying Wang, Kate Hunt, Irwin Nazareth, Nick Freemantle, and Irene Petersen published *Do Men Consult Less Than Women? An Analysis of Routinely Collected UK General Practice Data* (*BMJ Open*, 2013, Vol. 3, e003320; [DOI: 10.1136/bmjopen-2013-003320](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2013-003320)). Not a survey: actual consultation records for roughly **1.87 million men and 1.92 million women** registered at 446 UK practices in 2010. **The crude consultation rate was 32% lower in men than in women** (3,152 vs 4,607 consultations per 1,000 person-years). **The gap was widest between the ages of 16 and 60.** Taking pregnancy and contraception visits out shrank the gap but didn't close it.

*(Honesty note, the big one: among people already receiving medication for depression or for cardiovascular disease, the gap nearly disappeared. Men were only about 8% and 5% less likely to consult than comparable women. So once a man is diagnosed and in treatment, he mostly turns up. The researchers warn against assuming women simply consult more "in all circumstances and at all ages." My own reading, not theirs: the gap lives* before *the diagnosis, which is exactly where noticing early counts.)*

**In the waiting room.** Ashley Thompson, Yvonne Anisimowicz, Baukje Miedema, William Hogg, Walter Wodchis, and Kris Aubrey-Bassler published *The Influence of Gender and Other Patient Characteristics on Health Care-Seeking Behaviour: A QUALICOPC Study* (*BMC Family Practice*, 2016, Vol. 17, Article 38; [DOI: 10.1186/s12875-016-0440-0](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-016-0440-0)). **7,260 patients in 759 practices across all 10 Canadian provinces** were asked whether they'd go to their family doctor for 11 different health concerns. **Women said they would go to a greater extent than men, for both physical and mental concerns.** The gap was larger for mental health concerns (d = −0.31) than for physical ones (d = −0.18).

*(Honesty note: these are self-reported intentions from people already sitting in a doctor's waiting room, and the authors themselves say the effect sizes are small. Direction consistent, size modest.)*

**What it costs, worldwide.** The World Health Organization's *World Health Statistics 2019* ([WHO IRIS](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/324835); [WHO release](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-04-2019-uneven-access-to-health-services-drives-life-expectancy-gaps-who)) found that boys born in 2019 could expect to live **69.8 years and girls 74.2, a gap of 4.4 years**. **Of the 40 leading causes of death, 33 cut men's life expectancy more than women's.** In 2016, a 30-year-old man's probability of dying of a noncommunicable disease before 70 was **44% higher** than a woman's. And the WHO says it directly: **where men and women face the same disease, men often seek health care less.** In countries with widespread HIV, men are less likely to get tested and less likely to get treatment; male TB patients appear less likely to seek care.

*(Honesty note: WHO doesn't blame the whole gap on avoiding doctors. Smoking, drinking, road injuries, violence, and biology all play a part. And notice the reverse lesson in the same report: the gap is narrowest where women* lack *access to care. When care is there, women use it, and it buys them years.)*

**What hurts versus what kills.** Vedavati Patwardhan, Gabriela Gil, Luisa Sorio Flor, and colleagues published *Differences Across the Lifespan Between Females and Males in the Top 20 Causes of Disease Burden Globally: A Systematic Analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021* (*The Lancet Public Health*, 2024, Vol. 9, No. 5, e282–e294; [DOI: 10.1016/S2468-2667(24)00053-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(24)00053-7)). **Males carried a higher disease burden than females for 13 of the top 20 causes**, led by COVID-19, road injuries, and ischaemic heart disease, the conditions that kill. The authors call for prevention that recognises **"the different health-seeking patterns of males."**

*(Honesty note: the same study found women carry more of the burden from conditions people live with for years, such as low back pain, depression, headache disorders, and anxiety. Women aren't healthier across the board. They're less likely to die of what could have been caught.)*

## Part two: what I think

Here's my verdict, in my own voice.

Look at the shape of the evidence. Men don't consult less because they're never sick. Once they're diagnosed and on medication for something like depression or heart disease, they turn up nearly as often as women do. The difference sits in the quiet stretch before that: the weeks of "it's nothing," the symptom nobody mentions, the appointment never booked. That stretch is where women's habit does its work. She notices, she mentions it, she books, she goes back for the result. He waits for it to become undeniable.

That's not a different style. That's a missing habit.

And the cost isn't abstract. Thirty-three of the forty leading causes of death take more years from men. Heart disease, the biggest single contributor, is exactly the kind of thing that gets found when someone goes and gets checked. A woman's "I'll just get it checked" is not anxiety. It's maintenance. A man's "it'll pass" is not courage. It's a bet made with a body that other people depend on.

The focus groups tell you the most useful thing of all. Men *can* go. They go fast when it protects something they value more than their pride: their job, their performance. So give a man a reason that outranks his pride. The best reason is her. A body that serves has to be maintained, and maintenance is her domain because she's the one who actually does it.

So I'll say it plainly. **On health, women's way is the better way.** Not because women never ignore anything, and not because doctors are always right. Because women act on the early signal, and men act on the late one. Early beats late, every time. A sex that maintains its body is a sex that should set the standard for how bodies get maintained.

Men like to call their neglect "being tough," "not making a fuss," "getting on with it." Strip the costume off and it's simpler than that: **avoidance wearing armor.** The fix isn't to make a man anxious. The fix is to put his body under her standard of care, where it belongs, and let her habit decide when something gets checked.

None of this costs a man his confidence. It takes a confident man to say "something's off, I'm getting it looked at" without needing to perform indifference. The man who can't say it is the insecure one. Care is strength that doesn't need to prove itself.

## Part three: the female advantage, stated plainly

Real, sourced edges, no hedging:

- **Women see their doctor more in the working years.** In UK GP records, men's crude consultation rate was 32% lower than women's, with the gap widest from 16 to 60 (Wang et al., 2013).
- **Women are readier to seek care for the same concern.** Higher stated willingness to see the family doctor for physical and mental health concerns (Thompson et al., 2016).
- **Women don't wear delay as a badge.** Men's own accounts tie reluctance to an idea that men "should" hold off (O'Brien et al., 2005), and the research trend is men delaying when ill (Galdas et al., 2005).
- **Women live longer.** A 4.4-year life-expectancy lead at birth, with 33 of the 40 leading causes of death weighing more heavily on men (WHO, 2019), and a lower burden from the conditions that kill (Patwardhan et al., 2024).

Put together: a sex that notices sooner, gets checked sooner, and lives longer. That's what "taking care of yourself" means in everyday life.

## Where neglect hides

| What neglect says | What it really is | The better way (hers) |
|---|---|---|
| "It'll pass." | A bet with your body | Notice it, then check it |
| "It's nothing." | Not wanting to know | Ask someone who would know |
| "I don't want to make a fuss." | Pride wearing manners | A short message is not a fuss |
| "I'll just take something." | Self-medicating in the dark | Follow the leaflet, tell the doctor |
| "Real men get on with it." | A habit wearing a biology costume | Habits can be trained up |

## Close

Neglect is silence. Nothing hurts loudly enough yet, so nothing gets done, and men have spent centuries mistaking that silence for strength. Her care is attention. It's the steady habit of noticing what the body is saying and answering it while the answer is still small.

Live by her attention. Let your silence go.

> **«הדאגה שלה לפני ההזנחה שלי.»**
> *Her care before my neglect.*
